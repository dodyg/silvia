# Lumen field manual

This manual is for field leads and team members who operate Lumen kits during
the pilot. It covers the kit itself, daily operation, maintenance, safety and
what to do when something goes wrong. The field handbook covers the launch
schedule and the team structure; read it first.

## 1. The kit

A Lumen kit arrives in a sealed hard case with a tamper strip across the
latch. Do not open a kit whose strip is broken; report it to the depot and use
a spare. Inside the case you will find the lantern head, the mast in three
sections, the main battery, the solar charger with its cable, a pouch of spare
fuses, the radio and a laminated quick-start card.

The lantern head is the part that people see. It holds the light sensor, the
status beacon on top and the dimmer dial on the side. The mast sections screw
together by hand and should never be tightened with tools, because the thread
is aluminium and strips easily.

The main battery holds 40 amp hours and powers the lantern for about three
nights on the default brightness. It sits in the base of the case and
connects with a keyed plug that only fits one way. The spare battery pack that
each team carries separately is smaller and is meant for emergencies, not for
daily use.

## 2. Setting up

Choose flat, firm ground at least three metres from any building, tree or
overhead line. Open the case, lift out the base plate and fold out its three
legs until they click. Screw the first mast section into the base plate, then
the second and the third. Mount the lantern head last and turn it a quarter
turn clockwise until the locking pin drops.

Connect the main battery before you connect the solar charger. If you connect
the charger first, the charge controller can latch into a fault state that
only a depot reset clears. Lay the solar panel flat or tilted towards the sun,
never under the lantern itself, and keep the cable off any path where people
walk.

Switch on with the rocker on the base. The beacon on the lantern head shows
the state of the kit: steady green means charged and working, slow green
means charging, and red means a fault. The beacon blinks amber when the
battery charge drops below 15 percent, which is the signal to swap in the
spare pack or shut the lantern down for the night.

## 3. Daily operation

Each morning, the field lead walks every kit in the team's area and notes the
beacon colour, the battery reading on the base display and any damage to the
case or mast. The notes go into the daily log on the team tablet before 10:00
so that the depot can plan replacements the same day.

Teams check in with the depot by radio every two hours on channel 6 while
they are in the field. If a check-in is missed twice in a row, the depot calls
the field lead's phone, and after that it sends the nearest team to look.
Keep radio messages short: your team number, your location, and anything the
depot needs to act on.

Brightness is set with the dimmer dial. The default position is the third
mark, which gives the three nights of runtime quoted above. Turning the dial
to the fifth mark roughly halves the runtime. Only raise the brightness when
the local coordinator asks for it, for example during an evening event.

At the end of each shift, wipe the solar panel with the dry cloth from the
case. Dust on the panel is the most common reason for a kit that will not
charge, and it is far more common than a faulty charger.

## 4. Maintenance

Recalibrate the light sensor every 200 operating hours. The operating hours
are shown on the base display when you hold the rocker down for five seconds.
To recalibrate, cover the sensor with the black cap from the quick-start pouch,
hold the dimmer dial at the first mark for ten seconds, and remove the cap when
the beacon flashes white twice.

The spare fuses in the pouch are rated at 7.5 amps. Never fit a fuse with a
higher rating, even for a short time: the wiring in the mast is sized for 7.5
amps and will overheat. A blown fuse almost always means water has reached
the base, so dry the base before you replace it.

Firmware updates happen only at the depot, never in the field. If the base
display shows an update notice, ignore it and mention it in the daily log; the
depot will schedule the kit for a swap.

Inspect the mast threads once a week for dirt and dents. A dented section
must be replaced, not straightened. The depot keeps spare sections and sends
them out with the next supply run.

## 5. Storage and transport

Store kits indoors, out of direct sunlight, between 5 and 30 degrees Celsius.
Batteries that sit above that range lose capacity quickly and may swell. Kits
are always transported in their cases with the battery disconnected and the
mast taken down. Never stack more than three cases on top of each other.

When a kit will not be used for more than a week, charge the main battery to
about half and disconnect it. A fully charged battery left for weeks ages
faster than one stored half full.

## 6. Safety

Do not operate a kit during a thunderstorm. Take the lantern head off the mast
and lay the mast flat until the storm has passed. Keep children away from the
base, where the battery connector is exposed while the kit is open.

If a battery is swollen, hot to the touch or smells of solvent, do not move it
by hand. Clear the area, call the depot and wait for the battery handling team.
Swollen batteries are never sent back in the normal return crate.

## 7. Faults and returns

Most faults can be cleared by switching the kit off, waiting thirty seconds and
switching it on again. If the beacon is still red, write down the fault code
from the base display and call the depot on the radio. The depot will tell you
whether to keep the kit running, swap it, or bring it in.

A kit that cannot be fixed in the field is returned to the depot using the
process in the field handbook. Put a red tag on the case so that no other team
picks it up by mistake, and note the serial number in the daily log.
